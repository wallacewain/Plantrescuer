Rescue field guide

The 48-hour triage

What to do in the first two days after you bring home a plant that everyone else had written off — and the one thing almost everybody does that finishes it off.

The Plant Rescuer Aug 21, 2026 · 9 min read
214 38 Share

The trolley outside the back door, 4pm on a Tuesday. Everything on it was 50p.

There is a trolley outside the back door of almost every garden centre, and nobody wants you to look at it. It's where the stock goes when it stops being sellable: the dropped leaves, the white crust on the soil, the pot that's been sitting in its own drip tray for a fortnight. Last Tuesday there were eleven plants on ours. I paid four pounds for the lot.

Most of them will be fine. That's the part people find hard to believe. A plant that looks finished is usually a plant that has been kept alive badly for a long time, and the damage you can see is weeks old — it isn't happening now. What you do in the first forty-eight hours decides whether that stops being true.

Do this, in this order

  1. Put it somewhere on its own. Not with your other plants, not yet. Shop stock arrives with company — scale, thrips, fungus gnats — and two weeks of quarantine costs you nothing if you're wrong.
  2. Take it out of the pot. All the way out. Everything you need to know is under the soil, and no amount of looking at the leaves will tell you instead.
  3. Read the roots with your fingers. Healthy roots are firm and pale and they resist you. Rotten ones slide out of their own skin and smell like a pond. Dormant ones are dry and stiff and do neither.
  4. Cut what's actually dead. Rotten root, back to firm tissue. Leaves that have gone fully brown and crisp. Leave anything still holding any green — the plant is feeding on it.
  5. Repot into dry mix, one size down. Not up. A weakened root ball in a big pot sits in wet soil it can't drink, and that's the fastest way to lose one you'd already saved.
  6. Then leave it alone. Bright shade, no fertiliser, no misting, one light drink. Two weeks of nothing. This is the hard bit.
Most plants people bring me aren't dying of neglect. They're dying of a fortnight of enthusiastic care applied to the wrong problem.

Step six is where the rescues are lost. A plant that has been drowning does not want feeding, and a plant with a third of its roots cut off cannot use fertiliser — it just sits in the soil and burns what's left. The instinct to intervene is the thing you are actually rescuing it from.

A note on the ones that don't make it. Roughly one in five. If the stem is soft where it meets the soil, that's rot in the crown and there's no route back from it — take a cutting from the healthiest growth and let the rest go. I'll take the whole propagation method through next week's post.

Before it goes live

Everything in the mock-up is stand-in content. These are the five things worth deciding for real — the rest is Substack's own furniture and looks after itself.

The name and the line under it

“Notes from the plants everybody else gave up on” is a guess. The tagline does the most work of anything on the page — it's what a new reader reads before deciding.

Real photographs

The leaf tiles are placeholders. Before-and-afters are the whole draw here, so the first thing to swap in is a genuine trolley shot and a set of week-one / week-six pairs.

Byline and numbers

It's currently written by “@theplantrescuer” with an invented subscriber count. Substack hides the count until it flatters you — worth leaving off at the start.

Prices and currency

Shown as $6 monthly / $50 annual. Substack takes 10% plus Stripe fees. The posted cutting on the annual tier is a nice hook but it's a real postage commitment.

What's free and what isn't

The split here is: rescue write-ups free, the Plant Clinic paid. That's the decision that shapes the publication — worth running free-only for a few months first.

Publication schedule

Every other Thursday, stated on the page. A cadence you can hold beats a fast one you can't, and it's the promise readers actually judge you on.
